How I assess a website, and what the findings actually mean

The note you received cited one specific fault on one specific page. This explains how it was found, what else the same pass checks, and what repairing it involves — so you can judge the work before spending twenty minutes on a call.

What a repair involves

  • Find where it breaks. A broken button is usually a page that was renamed while the things pointing at it were left behind.
  • Fix it and the pattern. Repoint the link, redirect the old address, then sweep the rest of the site — a rename rarely breaks only one thing.
  • Confirm from outside. Re-run the same measurement, on the live site, not on a copy.
Most of what this finds is hours, not weeks. The expensive part is not the repair — it is the months a fault sits there without anyone reporting it, because people who hit it simply leave.

One finding, carried the whole way

What was seen

A visitor clicks Book online. The address behind the button no longer exists, so the server answers with a not-found page instead of the form.

→
Why it happens

Almost always a page that was renamed or moved, while the things pointing at it were left as they were. Nothing looks broken from the inside.

→
What it costs

The people who reach it had already decided. They do not ring to report it — they go back to the results and pick whoever is next.

→
What I would do

Repoint the button, redirect the old address so nothing else breaks, then sweep the site for the same pattern. Usually an afternoon.

What this is not

Not a scan report. An automated tool will hand you two hundred warnings sorted by severity. That list is why nobody acts on it. You were sent one fault because one is what a person can act on this week.

Nothing private was touched. Public pages only, robots.txt obeyed, no logins, no forms submitted, nothing stored beyond the pages themselves. Everything above is visible to anyone who visits your site.
