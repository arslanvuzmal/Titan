01The short answer

Seven things are happening at once. They are listed in order of how much throughput each one costs, and every number below came from the live database or the running worker's logs, not from the code's own claims about itself.

  1. A crash is eating three out of four research runs. Page text containing a lone Unicode surrogate — a broken emoji, a mangled character — is rejected by Postgres when the crawl is written to jsonb. The crawl succeeded; the save fails, all eight retries fail identically, and the lead is thrown away. 100 occurrences in the last 24 hours, all at activities/pipeline.py:271. This is the single largest loss in the system.
  2. Only 14% of crawled sites give up an email address. 623 addresses from 4,393 completed crawls. The pipeline was designed assuming a third. That gap is why 2,470 well-scored leads sit in manual_review with no contact at all — and nothing ever goes back for them.
  3. The repair job that rescues abandoned drafts has been dead for five days. The housekeeping schedule reports MissedCatchupWindow: 52 against 30 runs, and its next fire time is five days in the past. 596 validated drafts are stranded because of it. They expire on 31 August and are lost.
  4. Sending is spread across 23 campaigns and starves all of them. The workspace cap is 100 a day; the allocator divides it by health across 23 active campaigns and lands on 2 or 3 sends each — and 0 for three of them. Nothing can build momentum at that rate. Six more “active” campaigns are in research_only mode and can never send anything at all.
  5. The bounce rate is 19.7% and will destroy the domain. Gmail and Yahoo filter above roughly 2%. The cause is that a published website address counts as verified enough to send: 564 of 623 addresses were never actually checked against a mailbox, because the SMTP prober correctly declines to probe Google and Microsoft, which front most business mail.
  6. 154 finished messages were permanently cancelled over a footer. The postal address was added to the sender identities after those drafts were composed, so the send-time check found it configured but missing from the body and blocked every one. 28 more died for a missing one-click unsubscribe header. All were validated, approved messages.
  7. The Smartlead route is dead and still being attempted. The API key is rejected on every sequence read (37 permanent failures), and the carrier campaigns have four sequence steps where Titan requires exactly one. Meanwhile one healthy mailbox is pinned at 5 sends a day because the warm-up ramp last advanced on 19 August.
The thing to hold on to

Not one of these is a design failure. The gates, the evidence rule and the refusal logic all work exactly as built — several of these defects are the safety machinery correctly refusing to send flawed mail. What is missing is the layer above: nothing is watching the system's own yield and acting on it. 363 operator notifications are sitting unread, and every one of these problems announced itself in one.

The root cause underneath two of them

The stuck housekeeping job and the frozen warm-up ramp looked like two separate bugs. They are one: Titan runs on a laptop that sleeps. Housekeeping reports 52 missed windows because it fires hourly and the machine is not always awake. The ramp fires at 06:10 UTC — 07:10 your time — and simply never runs. Both jobs are correct; the host is not always there.

This proved itself while I was writing this page: Docker Desktop stopped mid-session and the entire stack went down. Nothing is sending as you read this. The container restart policies are right (unless-stopped on all fifteen), so they come back when Docker does — but a system that stops when a lid closes is not autonomous in any useful sense, no matter how good the gates are.

The one change that outranks everything

Move Titan to a host that does not sleep. It fixes the schedules, the ramp, the discovery gaps and the multi-day sending holes at once — and every other phase in this programme assumes it. A small always-on VPS is roughly $10–20 a month. Nothing else on this page compounds the way this does.

04What 40 a day actually costs

Before choosing what to build, it is worth being precise about what the target demands, because the arithmetic decides the plan.

Work backwards from a client. Say the goal is two qualified conversations a week. At a realistic cold-outreach positive-reply rate of 3–4%, that needs about 200 delivered messages a week — the 40 a day the system was designed around. Now push that number back up the funnel at today's conversion rates against what it would be after the fixes:

The point of that table

At today's rates, hitting the target would mean starting 1,373 research runs every day — roughly ten times the current pace, at ten times the crawl and model cost, to send forty emails. That is not a scaling problem, it is a leak. Fix the three critical defects and the same forty emails cost 130 runs. The target goes from unreachable to comfortable without adding a single machine.

This is also the answer to “why not just turn everything up”. Raising limits on a funnel that loses 99% of its input multiplies the waste, and the bounce rate would take the sending domain down long before the volume arrived.

Phase 1 — what the evidence changed

Phase 1 was aimed at the wrong thing and the data said so. Before tightening anything I measured bounce rate against verification status, and it does not predict bounces at all: published_first_party bounces at 6.1% and provider_verified at 7.4% — verified addresses bounce more. So W10 is revised (verification status is not the signal; role addresses are — 6.8% against 4.2% for named people) and W11, the paid verifier, is dropped. It would have bought a signal with no predictive power on this workspace. That is roughly $40 a month not spent and, more to the point, a fix that would not have worked.

The real bounce number is also lower than I reported: 5.8% lifetime (30 of 514), not 19.7%. The 19.7% came from a seven-day window of 61 sends, which was too small to mean anything. And two-thirds of what does fail is retryable — 32 soft against 17 permanent.

What was actually wrong is worse, and it was free to fix. Titan's own SMTP host answers a burst with 554 … too many messages from sender in last 60 minutes. 554 sat in PERMANENT_REPLY_CODES as “this recipient will never accept mail”, so every message caught by our own throttle was recorded as an invalid recipient and the business suppressed for good — 30 correctly-addressed practices retired because we sent too fast. The verifier had already learned this lesson on its first live run and grown a SENDER_REJECTION_MARKERS list; the delivery path never inherited it. One shared rule now serves both, with tests in both directions — a dead mailbox still suppresses, because reading a hard bounce as our fault would be the equally expensive mistake.

W19+ — the address now outlives the lead

The fix for what the sample test found, and it is a smaller change than the finding deserved: a below-threshold lead now resolves its contact before it is set down. Same activity as the happy path, same idempotency key, so a lead that later clears the bar finds its own prior work instead of resolving twice. The result is deliberately not read — a lead under the bar gets no draft, and reading the eligible channel here would invite a later edit that quietly turned a capture into an approach. Two assertions hold that: captured, and never drafted.

Why it is worth doing at all: the bar is campaign policy, not a verdict. The autonomy manager moves min_lead_score, and one live campaign already sits at 55 where the rest sit at 70. A lead scored 65 today becomes mailable the moment the bar moves — and without this, the only route back was crawling the whole site again.

Shipped carefully. 63 research workflows were in flight, and adding an activity call changes replay history. The narrow risk is a workflow sitting mid-close_research_run on exactly this branch, so the new call sits behind workflow.patched("capture-contact-below-threshold") rather than behind my own reasoning that it was probably safe. Deployed with zero non-determinism errors against those in-flight runs.

One existing test asserted the opposite — "resolve_contact" not in recorder.calls — because the old intent really was “do no further work”. It was rewritten to state the new rule and why, not quietly flipped.

W13 — the 7-a-day cap turns out to be correct

The planner reported “daily sending is bounded by mailbox warm-up — configured_limit 100, deliverable_today 7”, and warm-up turned out to be innocent: outreach@ and sales@ are both past it (day 21 and day 20, no cap), and only projects@ is legitimately warming at 7. The other two were health-blocked on a bounce rate built entirely from our own throttle.

I then talked myself into a tidy causal loop — throttle causes bounce causes block causes collapse — and it was wrong. The health window counts bounced_at, not state, and the throttled messages never set it. Clearing them changed nothing, which is how I found out. The block is legitimate: outreach@ sits at 5 hard bounces in 104 sends (4.8%) and sales@ at 3 in 98 (3.1%), all from genuine bounce reports, and both are over the pause threshold on a window that small.

So the 7-a-day cap is the system working, not a bug. The way out is not to override the health gate; it is to stop generating genuine bounces. The addresses doing it name the cause — recruitment@zenlaw.co.uk and 0606info@207dentalcare.com, a hiring inbox and a phone number fused to a mailbox — both shapes the contacts module already describes and neither of which should have been written to. That is address quality — so I went to fix it, and found it already fixed.

Both guards exist. recruitment@ is in the never-contact list (the contacts module names recruitment@zenlaw.co.uk in its own comments as the case that put it there), and DIGIT_RUN_PREFIX catches a mailbox with a phone number fused to the front — it requires three or more digits precisely so 3dprint@ survives and 0606info@ does not. Both bounces predate their guard.

So I audited the stored data rather than assume: all 633 active contact channels pass the current rules, with no legacy addresses left that either guard would now refuse. There is no address-quality work outstanding, the 8 bounces are historical, and the health block will clear on its own as the window ages. Writing a fix here would have been inventing work the codebase had already done.

The repair that was warranted still stands. Every one of the 31 throttled addresses was checked for genuine 5xx evidence first; none had any. 31 wrongly-suppressed businesses restored and 31 false bounce states corrected to failed — never delivered, never bounced. Hard-bounce suppressions fell 47 → 16, and the 16 that remain trace to real bounce reports and were left alone. That part was right; the claim I hung on it was not.

Mailboxes are not the bottleneck — contact supply is

Two of three mailboxes are already past warm-up at 50 a day each. Nominal capacity is ~107/day against a 40/day target, and the workspace cap is 100. A fourth and fifth mailbox would add roughly 100 more — capacity that has nothing to send.

The real ceiling is upstream: ~47 new contactable addresses a day against a 40-a-day target, which is parity with no margin. Five mailboxes would not move the number; Phase 2's yield work would. Each new mailbox also costs 21 days of ramp before it reaches 50 — the curve runs 0.10 → 1.00 of target over three weeks.

D18 & D19 fixed — the reply path can now tell us from a prospect

A new first question in the classifier, ahead of every other rule: was there a recipient at all? Everything downstream answers “what did this person mean”, which is a confident reading of a sentence nobody outside the system wrote. ReplyKind.NOT_A_PROSPECT covers our own sending identities, the operator's mailbox, and reciprocal warm-up traffic. It stops nothing and suppresses nothing.

Our addresses are read live from the database, so a mailbox added to the pool is covered the moment it exists rather than when somebody remembers to list it — which matters given you are about to add two. The operator's own address is new configuration (TITAN_OPERATOR_EMAIL), because that one is not a sender identity and nothing else knew it.

Warm-up detection is textual and therefore a heuristic — flame-smile and model-using are randomly drawn spintax and the list cannot be complete. Worth having anyway: every one it catches is a reply-rate figure that stops being wrong, and a miss just leaves today's behaviour.

14Two hard truths

Two items on your list will not work the way the industry says they do, and I would rather say so now than build them and have you find out from the numbers.

1 · “Have they seen it” is mostly unanswerable now

Open tracking works by embedding an invisible image; when the mail client loads it, you record an open. Since 2021, Apple Mail pre-loads that image for every message before the human sees anything — on iPhone, iPad and Mac. Gmail proxies images through its own cache. Between them that is the majority of the inboxes you are writing to, and it means a reported “open” is frequently a machine, while a real reader who blocks images registers nothing at all. Building it would give you a number that looks like insight and is closer to noise, and I would rather not put a metric on your dashboard that quietly lies.

What I would build instead is click tracking, on the portfolio link already in every message. A click cannot be faked by a pre-fetcher in the same way, it is a decision by a person, and it is the signal that actually predicts a reply. It needs one public HTTPS endpoint — which is a real decision, because right now nothing in Titan is exposed to the internet, and I know you have turned down a public tunnel before. Two honest options: a small always-on endpoint on a host you control, or accept reply-and-bounce as the only engagement signals and skip this entirely. The system works either way; it just learns slower without it.

2 · Boom detection needs a source, and the best one is already yours

There is no feed that says “dentists in Leeds are booming”. Anyone selling you one is selling a guess. But you are already buying the raw material: Google Places hands back a review count and rating for every business you look at, and you already crawl the winners' websites. Cohort statistics over those two things — how many businesses are brand new, how fast reviews are accumulating, and what share of the vertical still has no booking system — is a genuine growth signal, computed from data you pay for anyway. Phase 6 is built entirely on that, with no new vendor.

The caveat worth stating: velocity needs two scans separated by time. I assumed that meant a cold start — it does not. Both fields are already in the Places search mask you pay for and already stored on every location row, so there is a usable baseline going back to 17 August for every vertical you have touched. Phase 6 produces a real signal on its first run for existing markets, and only genuinely new verticals have to wait.

What is already strong

Evidence-first research, per-industry vocabulary so a solicitor and a gym owner get genuinely different prose, deterministic scoring you can argue with, a send gate that fails closed, exactly-once delivery, warm-up, adaptive limits, statistical A/B testing that reports “inconclusive” honestly, and a bounded autonomy layer that can only ever be more conservative than you. This is a better foundation than most commercial tools in this space have.

The four real gaps

  • It has never had a conversation. Everything is optimised up to the first message. Expertise in converting leads lives in the second, third and fourth exchange — handling “how much”, “send me examples”, “we already have someone”. Phase 5 is not a feature, it is the missing half of the product.
  • Nothing learns. Every learning mechanism is written and inert, because they all need a reply rate and there has never been one. The moment Phases 0–3 produce volume, this stops being a build problem and becomes a switch.
  • It cannot see itself. A system with 363 unread alarms about its own broken parts is not autonomous regardless of how many gates it passes. Phase 4.
  • It cannot choose its own market. Every vertical and city in the database was typed by a human. Until the system picks where to hunt, it is an extremely capable tool being pointed — not an operator. Phase 6.
The honest read

You do not need a smarter system. You need the one you have to stop losing 99% of its work, to stop bouncing a fifth of what it sends, and to be able to hold a conversation. The intelligence is already there and it has never had enough traffic to demonstrate it.
